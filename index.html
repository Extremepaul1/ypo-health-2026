his weekend was built on a simple premise: the healthcare system was designed to save your life, not to keep you healthy — and nobody is going to manage your health KPIs for you. Over two days in Halifax, a surgeon-turned-longevity physician, a psychiatrist, a behaviour scientist, an ER doctor, a sleep therapist and two hormone physicians made the case, from eight different angles, for running your biology like the most important company you'll ever own.

What follows is the record — not the AI-generated summaries that shipped with the recordings, but each session read in full and checked. Where two speakers disagreed, both views are kept and the argument gets its own section, because that's where the science is still open and your own goals decide.

Every attendee question, story and personal health detail has been removed, including the hosts'. The closing conversation was framed on stage as YPO-only and confidential, so only the physicians' points appear.

Three sessions

Friday morning · Keynote

Dr. Darshan Shah

Board-certified surgeon · CEO and founder, Next Health · host of the Extend podcast · YPO Global Health & Wellness Chair

“Nobody is going to manage your health KPIs for you. Own the data, use the new diagnostics early, and start now.”

  • 6 tools
    under $500 total: bioimpedance scale, BP cuff, blood panel, wearable, CGM, grip dynamometer
  • 45 min
    the sitting limit — inflammatory markers rise after it; move every 45 minutes
  • 53 → 28
    his biological age at 40, then at 54 — “start now” is the whole message
All twelve takeaways
  1. The Four P's (from Hood & Price, The Age of Scientific Wellness): Predictive (biomarkers before symptoms), Preventive (diagnose at stage one, when it's curable), Personalized (n-of-1 — your own data beats the population bell curve), Participatory (daily, and done by you, not your doctor or insurer). “Health is a habit, and it's a daily habit.”
  2. Six tools, under $500 total. Bioimpedance scale (track skeletal muscle mass, not weight — “throw your scale in the trash”; build muscle and fat follows). Blood-pressure cuff (first thing in the morning, three readings, averaged; closer to 120/70 is best; every minute over 130/80 damages organs; the Hilo wrist monitor is available in Canada). Blood tests (about ten markers that matter, tracked over time). A wearable for HRV, sleep and stress. A continuous glucose monitor to learn your own food and meal-order responses. A grip dynamometer (~$15) — grip strength tracks whole-body strength and mortality; be at least the 50th percentile for your age and sex.
  3. Nutrition, Pareto version. Ultra-processed food is the first cut: every 10% of daily calories from UPF raised all-cause mortality about 15% in the 207,291-person meta-analysis his workbook cites. Flip the old food pyramid: 500 g+ of varied vegetables a day, protein at 1.5–2 g/kg split across meals from well-sourced meat and fish (quality whey or pea powder is fine), good fats (olive oil, avocado, salmon). Eat fibre first, then protein. No packaged snacks; no high-carb breakfasts, which dysregulate glucose all day.
  4. Metabolic health is the hidden epidemic. About 90% of Americans are metabolically unhealthy and Canada is “not far off.” The marker is HbA1c, a three-month blood-sugar average: 6.5+ is diabetic, 5.7–6.5 pre-diabetic, and his stage target is under 5.4 (his workbook says under 5.2). GLP-1s are for reversing metabolic disease, not just weight; they only work well paired with more protein, more fibre and heavy resistance training.
  5. Movement: don't be sedentary, then lift. After 45 minutes of sitting, inflammatory markers rise; four-plus sedentary hours a day carries the same 15% mortality penalty as UPF. After 40 you are programmed to lose muscle, and it falls off a cliff around 70 — strength training comes first: compound lifts, each set close to failure, about 45 minutes three times a week. Then cardio for VO2 max (aim for the 40s after age 40) via sprint intervals — e.g. 30 s on / 90 s off, four to six rounds, heart rate into zone 4 (~150–160). On stage he said he does no zone 2 for lack of time; his workbook prescribes 30 minutes a day of it.
  6. Sleep: track it before assuming it's fine. Patients who “sleep great” turn out to wake 30 times a night with apnea. Apnea is non-negotiable to treat. Bedroom as a cold, dark cave. Charge your phone in another room — even powered off beside you, the brain associates it with stress.
  7. Functional medicine looks for root causes (Bland, The Disease Delusion): inflammation, hormone dysregulation, gut dysbiosis, chronic stress, insulin resistance, toxic exposure, mitochondrial dysfunction. Ninety percent of the immune system lives behind a one-cell-thick gut wall that UPF, sugar, antibiotics and NSAIDs damage. Track hsCRP (under 1, ideally near zero). Autoimmune conditions like Hashimoto's are often gut- and gluten-driven and are not automatically lifelong. speaker's view
  8. Hormones were vilified by flawed science. His position: everyone should talk to a physician skilled in hormone therapy. For men he wants testosterone above 400 ng/dL at minimum and optimizes toward ~900; most peri- and post-menopausal women he knows are on estrogen and progesterone “to their benefit.” speaker's view
  9. Toxins, one a week. Air: open windows, purifier in the bedroom and office. Water: reverse osmosis under the sink, glass not plastic. Food and cosmetics: scan with the Yuka app and switch products. Shoes off at the door. His 52-week elimination guide is in the workbook. Alcohol: the liver clears about one drink every two hours — keep it to one or two drinks on two or three days a week. Stress is a toxin too: break every 45 minutes and watch your HRV trend upward.
  10. Supplements: a core stack, not six baggies. Vitamin D3 + K2, a third-party-tested omega-3 kept in the fridge, magnesium, a methylated B-complex, creatine 5–10 g a day (muscle and cognition) verify, plus fibre and protein powder as needed. Vet brands with the SuppCo app.
  11. Hyper-preventive diagnostics. Half of heart-attack victims didn't know they had heart disease. Replace LDL with ApoB (80 or under on stage; under 90 in the workbook) and get a coronary calcium score CT — it cancelled his brother's Kilimanjaro climb by finding a massive blockage. Standard screening covers four or five cancers; full-body MRI and liquid biopsy (Galleri) look for the rest. For the brain: know your APOE status, and the new blood markers (amyloid ratio, p-tau217) show whether you're heading toward cognitive impairment while there is still time to change course.
  12. Habit mechanics. A 30-minute morning routine (Elrod's Miracle Morning: ten minutes each physical, mental, spiritual) has done more for him than any peptide; habits stack. Stay healthy long enough to benefit from digital biology, GLP-1-class signal therapeutics, a coming type 1 diabetes cure and Kurzweil's projected longevity escape velocity around 2040.
If you do one thing

Buy the six tools and book a coronary calcium score.

Books: Atomic Habits, Miracle Morning, The Disease Delusion, The Age of Scientific Wellness, Food: What the Heck Should I Eat?, Glucose Revolution, Good Energy, Bredesen on Alzheimer's. Friday late morning · Mental fitness

Dr. Jackie Kinley, MD

Psychiatrist · Atlantic Institute for Resilience · runs the provincial trauma program · served through Columbine and 9/11

“Resilience is a trainable skill, not a trait. The brain is a muscle and stress is the barbell — the goal is capacity, not the absence of stress.”

  • 5 zones
    green, yellow, red, purple, blue — each with its own thought, feeling and behaviour signature
  • 5–7 days
    in one zone is “stuck”; healthy is moving through them within hours
  • 4 muscles
    Think Fit, Feel Fit, Act Fit, C-Fit — all measurable, all trainable
All twelve takeaways
  1. Stress sits on a curve. Too little: apathy, “rusting out” toward retirement. The sweet spot: focus, growth. Too much for too long: frustration, overload, burnout. Her reframe of retirement: don't retire, rewire.
  2. Mental illness as inflammation of the brain. Chronic stress raises TNF and suppresses BDNF; after mental-fitness training her hospital cohorts showed BDNF up, TNF down, and fMRI shows the neural patterns change. Labels are less useful than the question: what's stuck?
  3. Emotions are not symptoms. Fear, anger and sadness are normal and necessary; the symptom is the stuck version — stuck fear is anxiety, stuck sadness is depression. “Grieving is healthy. Depression is stuck sadness.” Emotion is energy and has to move through you; suppressed, it lands in the body.
  4. Four cardinal emotions, like colours from one light. Joy (north), sadness (south — it means something mattered), anger (east — “anger is hope, forward motion”), fear (west — protects what matters). All come from love and attachment. Your emotions are also your moral compass: shame and guilt signal you're acting out of line with your values.
  5. Build your own stress barometer. Five zones, each with a signature in thoughts, feelings and behaviour: green (joy; optimistic, social), yellow (fear; watchful, tense), red (anger; critical, blaming, argumentative), purple (panic; trapped, desperate), blue (hopelessness; sad, withdrawn). Healthy is moving through zones within hours; stuck is five to seven days. Check in with yourself — not your wearable — several times a day.
  6. Internal stressors outrank external ones. Workload, markets and customers you can influence but not control. What hurts most is internal: perfectionism, hyper-responsibility, refusing to delegate, over-identifying with the business (“if it fails, I'm a failure”), not being able to ask for help. “The most important relationship you'll ever have is the one with yourself.”
  7. Why founders crash after a sale. The business was a piece of their identity, sometimes all of it; the exit is a loss. Fear, anger and sadness follow, and un-grieved loss becomes depression. Keep the business a piece of you, never the whole.
  8. Compassion is renewable — if you refill it. High empathy with low self-compassion is the compassion-fatigue profile: you give all day and arrive home empty and bitter. Strengths overused become liabilities. A healthy boundary sounds like “I don't have space for this right now; let's come back to it.”
  9. Four mental muscle groups, all measurable and trainable. Think Fit (executive function, clear decisions under pressure), Feel Fit (reading the body's signals and tolerating emotion — emotions live in the body, not the head), Act Fit (social intelligence; staying in relationship under stress), C-Fit (the association cortex; keeping perspective and proportion). The QR snapshot gives a profile; the full 36-page assessment comes with a one-hour coaching debrief. The centre score is a proxy for your overall resource level, not a diagnosis.
  10. Resilience inside out. Under stress the instinct is to speed up and narrow; the skill is to slow, ground, regulate, then re-engage. Strength plus flexibility — “we don't want walls, we want doors.”
  11. Phones are alienators. A phone on the dinner table takes half of someone's attention; undivided attention is the most valuable gift you can give. Your brain is analog — the phone is RAM, and nothing gets into long-term memory if you never slow down.
  12. Don't bubble-wrap people. Kids and employees build capacity through graded stress with support, like adding weight to a bar; remove all stress and they can't cope at 17 or at work. Discussion from the floor added Dr. Alex Russell's rule: unless it's catastrophic, let the natural consequences happen. Entrepreneurs show a disproportionately high incidence of depression, anxiety and mood disorders — the training exists because the need is real.
If you do one thing

Take the snapshot, then write down what you think, feel and do in each of the five zones so you can catch yourself earlier.

Resources. Her book Mental Fitness: The Game Changer (in the gift bag); the resilience snapshot via the session QR code; the full assessment with coaching debrief; AIR's digital “brain gym.”

Friday afternoon · Behaviour change

Tim Jones

CEO, Precision Health (formerly Precision Nutrition) · physics, exercise physiology, nutritional biochemistry and behaviour science · 350,000+ coaches and clients in 140 countries

“More information rarely fixes anything. The gap is between knowing and doing, and the skill that closes it is adapting, not discipline.”

  • 10–49%
    consistency still lost weight, fat and inches in their 1,000-client, million-data-point analysis
  • +8 g fibre
    a day carried ~27% lower all-cause mortality in the trial he cited — the minimum effective dose
  • 9 / 10
    ready, willing and able — or shrink the action until you are
All twelve takeaways
  1. The data isn't the hard part. His own 1,500-day tracking program halved his cholesterol and landed him in Men's Health — and he does not recommend anyone do what he did. All the testing in the world couldn't decide whether to have a drink with friends or whether to train early or sleep in. “My plan needed to fit my life.”
  2. Adherence is the only factor consistent across all the research on weight loss and disease outcomes. Even people at 10–49% consistency lost weight, fat and inches. The best diet is the one you'll stick to.
  3. Three things get in the way, from a database of 700,000 coaching conversations: all-or-nothing thinking, self-worth (“I'm terrified I'm not doing enough”), and competing demands. Success starts with the life behind the behaviour.
  4. Make the action smaller (BJ Fogg, Stanford): if a behaviour is hard, motivation has to be very high and it doesn't last; if it's easy, low motivation is enough. Design for Tuesday — the meeting runs late, you fly tomorrow, dinner is waiting and you're tired. A plan that only works on a good day is not a plan.
  5. Minimum effective dose. Trial-specific results he showed, not promises for everyone: +8 g fibre a day, ~27% lower all-cause mortality; consistent bed and wake times, close to 50% lower; a 5–10 minute slow jog, ~30% lower all-cause and 45% lower cardiovascular mortality; 13 minutes of guided meditation, better memory and attention; 30–60 minutes of strength a week, ~20% lower all-cause mortality. “Our bar does not need to be perfection. It needs to be consistency.”
  6. The 80/20 order (his illustration, not a study): movement, nutrition, sleep, stress management, relationships, testing — and only then supplements and biohacks. “Creatine is not going to change your life.” Bloodwork tells you which of the big four to attack first, and fixing one tends to move every marker.
  7. Scale down, don't skip. 60-minute gym session becomes 15 minutes of bodyweight in a hotel room; full meal prep becomes protein and produce ready to grab; eight hours of sleep becomes the same bed and wake time; 20-minute meditation becomes three minutes of breathwork before a meeting. “Integrate into your life, don't add to it.”
  8. The cost of optimization. Stack every protocol from every podcast (his article with trainer Ben Bruno) and you'd have to quit your job, leave your marriage and stop sleeping. YPO members are the worst offenders for “if I can't do it all, I won't do it.”
  9. Identity drives behaviour. “I am someone who…” — the person you identify as does the actions. From the room: “I meditate every morning” became “I am someone who is calm and inherently happy.” Rate your readiness, willingness and ability out of 10; if any is below 9, shrink the action.
  10. The 90-day worksheet. What do you want to keep being able to do? Your 90-day goal and the constraint holding it back. “After I [trigger], I will [small action].” How you'll adapt when life gets messy (the “Life Happens” cards). What you'll track, your two-week review date, and who checks in.
  11. Stress you can't feel shows up in data. His CGM spiked not from food but from a creative meeting, a private-equity meeting and a disagreement with his wife — cortisol he had no story for. In-for-four, out-for-six breathing moved his HRV; the point is act, review, adjust.
  12. From the Q&A. Sleep beats exercise in the stack rank, but consistency of timing is trumping total hours in recent research (target still 7–8; check bloodwork and how you feel before chasing a number). Alcohol: there is no research-established “safe” level, but the depth of your relationships is the strongest longevity predictor (Harvard), and rigid abstinence can cost you those — be conscientious and tie it to your goals and bloodwork. THC: cognitive effects appear at two or more uses a week. GLP-1s: without protein, strength training and sleep, up to ~30% of the weight lost can be lean mass.
If you do one thing

Pick one action from this weekend, shrink it until you're a 9/10 ready, willing and able, and attach it to something you already do every day.

Resources. The 90-day worksheet and Life Happens cards from the session; Precision Health coaching. The chapter's pre-event survey: strong on moderate activity, never-smoked, social life and sense of purpose; the gaps were vegetables, sleep, stress management, protein, strength training and vigorous cardio.

Five sessions

Saturday 8:30 · Screening and the system

Dr. John Gillis

Co-founder, Signature Health · founder, 24Care · family and emergency medicine, chronic pain · Dartmouth General ER · Atlantic Registry licence

“Do the big, boring things regularly, know what you take, have a plan for when you're sick — and don't end up in my ER.”

  • 8 hours
    an ER wait, and that “is generous.” Go for chest pain, stroke signs, major trauma, severe allergy — nothing else
  • 1–2 yrs
    between full check-ups — “like taking the car in”
  • 1 question
    before any test: will the result change treatment, prevention or management?
All ten takeaways
  1. The ER is the worst of the worst. Roughly 16 million visits a year verify scope; an eight-hour wait “is generous”; the one-patient-one-record system has slowed everything and hurt morale. Go for chest pain, stroke signs, major trauma or bleeding, and severe allergic reactions. For everything else, have a better plan.
  2. Know your medications. What you take, why, and how it interacts with other pills, food and alcohol.
  3. The screening checklist. A full check-in every one to two years. Women: mammogram, Pap every three years, bone density. Men: PSA after 50, yearly to 70 unless high-risk; prostate exam every two years. Everyone: blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, atrial fibrillation. Colon: stool test every two years after 50; colonoscopy only with family history, blood, symptoms or a genetic syndrome — it carries a perforation risk. Chest X-ray is “basically useless” as a screen; low-dose CT for lung cancer only with 25+ pack-years.
  4. Don't over-test. “Incidentalomas” — a benign kidney cyst on a scan you didn't need — create worry without changing anything. His rule for any test: will the result change treatment, prevention or management?
  5. Have a backup plan. A family doctor or someone you can see quickly. “You have the best accountants, the best lawyers, the best insurance — and you do a really poor job of taking care of yourself.” Then extend the same thinking to the people driving the forklift.
  6. Bloodwork frequency. Provincial rules cap some tests (PSA no more than every three months); Signature does its blood work privately rather than bill the province.
  7. AI in the exam room. Useful — “Dr. Google is now Dr. AI” — for patient education and preparation, as long as it's one piece of the puzzle, read with context.
  8. The system, candidly. Private care is here to stay; the big things — cardiac, cancer — are done very well in Halifax; elective hips and cataracts are badly backlogged; neurology waits are three years and gynecology 18–24 months even for serious cases, so navigation matters and sometimes the honest answer is Boston or Mayo. The productivity model broke: one retiring physician carried 4,000–5,500 patients at 55–60 hours a week; new doctors carry 1,200–1,400 at 35–40 hours, so it takes three or four to replace one, and salary-only pay removed the incentive. His fix: ask every nurse, paramedic and cleaner how they'd be more efficient and incentivize it — “get my CT back in an hour, not six.”
  9. What actually prevents what he sees. “Eat better, be more active, sleep more, drink less — basically, have no fun.”
  10. GLP-1s: a sceptic converted. He wanted patients in the gym first; the results changed his mind — sustained weight loss, better habits, and anecdotally less craving for alcohol and cigarettes. Long-term data is still needed and the baseline lifestyle still matters. Physical improvement drives mental health.
If you do one thing

Book the one-to-two-year full check and write down, today, what you'll do the next time you're sick at 3 a.m.

Resources. Signature Health (executive health, private bloodwork); 24Care (on-demand access).

Saturday morning · Sleep

Michèle Morrison, RRT

Registered respiratory therapist · managing partner, New Brunswick Avant Respiratory

“Sleep isn't one of three pillars beside food and exercise — it's the foundation under both. Most ‘sleep problems’ are nervous-system problems wearing a disguise.”

  • 7 hours
    of actual sleep — currently the best longevity signal; not eight, not nine
  • 45 min
    the worst possible nap length: you wake from deep sleep groggy. Take 20–30, or the full 90
  • 4–6 hrs
    caffeine's half-life — an evening coffee is “starting the match with eight players”
All thirteen takeaways
  1. The badge of honour has flipped. A decade ago people bragged about four hours; now they ask how to get seven. Seven hours is associated with the best longevity outcomes — though about 5% genuinely need 10–11 and a few are wired for six.
  2. The vicious circle starts innocently: one late night, skip the gym, extra coffee, no evening walk, cravings for easy carbs, five pounds, worse sleep.
  3. Two systems have to peak together. The circadian clock runs on routine and light: get morning light, kill evening light, use night mode on every screen from about 8 p.m., and accept the post-lunch dip as physiology. Sleep pressure runs on adenosine building through the day; caffeine sits on those receptors for a 4–6 hour half-life. Wide awake after dozing on the couch? One system fired without the other.
  4. A body-temperature drop triggers sleep. A hot bath or shower one to two hours before bed pushes blood to the skin so you cool faster afterward — it's the drop, not the bath.
  5. Jet lag and melatonin. Expect roughly 50 minutes of adjustment a day, so four time zones is four to five days. Melatonin is a hormone, not a supplement: it works for circadian disruption like jet lag and does very little for ordinary insomnia; it's not indicated for children, and high doses simply sedate. Even the one-hour daylight-saving shift brings ~10% more motor-vehicle accidents.
  6. Sleep architecture. About five complete ~90-minute cycles make a restorative night. Stage 1 is light and semi-aware. Stage 2 consolidates motor skills. Stage 3, deep sleep, does physical repair, immune work and glymphatic clearing of the brain, and is prioritized early in the night. REM — nearly an awake brain in a paralysed body — handles emotional processing and dominates the second half, which is why waking an hour early costs about a third of your REM. Alcohol: more stage 1, less stage 3 and REM. “Passing out is not sleeping.”
  7. How to nap. Twenty to thirty minutes, alarm set, allowing five to ten minutes to wind down; earlier is better. Forty-five minutes is the worst possible length. If you were up all night, take the full 90-minute cycle instead.
  8. Insomnia, honestly. Chronic means three-plus nights a week for three-plus months. True primary insomnia is rare; most is secondary to habits, stress and “insufficient sleep syndrome.” Seven hours means seven hours of sleep, not seven hours of bed with reading and shows inside it.
  9. The self-treatment trap. “Sedation is not sleep.” Prescription and over-the-counter aids alter brain activity rather than restore it, lose about half their effect after two weeks, and are designed for short-term use.
  10. CBT-I is the gold standard almost everyone skips. Cognitive restructuring (“even if I don't sleep, I'll be okay”), sleep restriction to push efficiency above 85%, and stimulus control — if you're not asleep, get out of bed and do something boring in the dark until sleepy. One change at a time. Sleepworks in Nova Scotia runs a six-week program.
  11. It's a regulation problem. Modern stress is constant and non-physical, but the body responds as if a sabre-toothed tiger were in the room. Learn active relaxation, not passive: the physiological sigh (inhale, second short inhale, long exhale) drops heart rate quickly; doom-scrolling is not rest.
  12. Wearables, with reservations. A bad score first thing validates a bad day before it starts; “orthosomnia” is real. Retirees beware: the 6 a.m. wake-up is ingrained, and a nap to “make up for it” teaches the body it can misbehave.
  13. The chapter's sleep study. Seventy-four WatchPAT studies, for education not diagnosis: roughly 56% in the normal range, 31% mild, 8% moderate and under 3% severe verify. Everyone has some apnea; 0–5 events an hour is normal. On your report: pAHI is the index; RDI above 20 is significant; hypoxic burden is time under-oxygenated. Untreated apnea causes — not just correlates with — low testosterone, type 2 diabetes and hypertension. Anyone with low testosterone should have their sleep checked before starting hormones.
If you do one thing

Protect seven hours of actual sleep at a fixed wake time, and move your last caffeine to before noon.

Resources. Your WatchPAT report and her reading guide; Sleepworks (Nova Scotia) for CBT-I; Michèle is in the chapter WhatsApp group for follow-up questions.

Saturday split · Men's session

Dr. Baldeep Bains

Halifax physician · University of Dundee · 16 years Royal Navy · hormone optimization, Harpal Clinic London

“There is no male menopause — just a slow decline you don't notice until you reverse it. Low testosterone is a finding, not a diagnosis.”

  • 6 markers
    on a morning draw: total T, free T, SHBG, LH, FSH, prolactin — not just a total
  • 5,000+ men
    in the TRAVERSE trial: major cardiac events were lower on TRT than placebo; the FDA dropped its warning
  • ~3 months
    for your own production to return after stopping — assume TRT is long-term
All eleven takeaways
  1. Levels are falling, and it isn't just age. Drivers: obesity and visceral fat (which converts testosterone to estrogen), poor sleep and apnea, chronic stress (a “hormone steal” toward cortisol), sedentary living, medications (opioids, many psychiatric drugs), and endocrine disruptors — BPA and plastics, cadmium, mercury, lead, pesticides.
  2. What testosterone actually does. Brain: mood, focus, less anxiety and brain fog. Metabolic: builds muscle, improves insulin sensitivity. Sexual: libido, erection and orgasm quality. Blood: raises hemoglobin — an altitude-training effect. Bone: density, partly via conversion to estrogen — men need estrogen. He does not recommend progesterone for men.
  3. The symptoms are not specific. Low libido, fewer morning erections, fatigue, irritability, brain fog, slow recovery, belly fat — all of which also show up in sleep apnea, depression, alcohol excess, thyroid disease and medication side effects. “You don't build muscle in the gym; you build it when you sleep.”
  4. Reversible causes first. Sleep (get apnea assessed), resistance training of the big groups, clean higher-protein nutrition, stress reduction, less alcohol — and weight loss, one of the strongest natural levers. He asks for commitment before a trial of treatment.
  5. Testing done properly. Ideally two early-morning draws. Canadian reference ranges: bioavailable 2.5–10 nmol/L, total 8–32 nmol/L. “The numbers are a guide, not a verdict” — a man who lived at 800 can be genuinely symptomatic at 500 and still be “in range.” He aims for the upper end, or slightly above, when the patient feels optimal. speaker's view
  6. How it's given. Compounded creams and gels (150–200 mg/g): steady levels, daily application. Injections: testosterone cypionate, ~250 mg every two weeks to start; smaller, more frequent doses are more stable. Pellets: no — too variable, can't be adjusted, infection risk. Patches and oral forms absorb poorly.
  7. The safety picture changed. TRAVERSE (NEJM; 5,000+ men aged 45–80) found major adverse cardiovascular events lower on TRT than placebo, and the FDA removed its black-box cardiovascular warning. No evidence TRT causes prostate cancer or worsens BPH; he monitors PSA. Statins can plausibly lower testosterone, since cholesterol is the precursor.
  8. Side effects to actually watch. Higher hematocrit; acne, oily skin and hair loss in a minority; over-dosing converts the excess to estrogen — fluid retention, flushing, tender breasts — which is why he won't use aromatase inhibitors. Testicular shrinkage as the brain's signal shuts off (beta-hCG can preserve it). AF and PE are rare findings. speaker's view
  9. Fertility and stopping. Sperm count drops on TRT; for most men it recovers within about three months of stopping, but not for everyone. Young men who want children should be counselled carefully. Own production takes up to three months to return.
  10. Monitoring. Ideally bloodwork every three months; he does six under provincial limits. About 95% of his TRT patients never want to stop.
  11. Finding the right doctor is the hard part. TRT gets almost no time in medical school; many GPs dismiss a low-normal result. He treats only his own roster for now and is working toward a private TRT clinic with Signature Health; Dr. Gillis's Atlantic licence covers members across the region in the meantime.
If you do one thing

Before asking anyone for testosterone, get your sleep studied and your waist down — then get a proper morning panel, not just a total T.

Resources. The TRAVERSE trial (NEJM, 2023); Signature Health.

Saturday split · Women's session

Dr. Kiran Bains

Family physician, women's health · monthly menopause clinic at Signature Health · notes from her slides; the session was not recorded

“Menopause is normal — 100% of women who live long enough go through it — but suffering through it is optional. The 2026 conversation about hormones is not the 2002 one.”

  • 4–8 yrs
    a typical transition; ~80% get hot flashes or night sweats; 1 in 10 may leave work over unmanaged symptoms
  • < 60
    or within 10 years of menopause — the window where MHT's benefit–risk is favourable for healthy women
  • No blood test
    needed after 45 — diagnosis is clinical. “We treat symptoms, not a number on a piece of paper”
All fourteen takeaways
  1. It hits at the worst time and gets normalized. The question is not “is this normal?” but “is it affecting my life?” If it touches sleep, work, relationships, sex, confidence or daily function, it's reasonable to seek help.
  2. Three stages. Perimenopause can start up to 10 years before the last period — the “zone of chaos,” where hormones swing high and low and pregnancy is still possible. Menopause is a single point: 12 months without a period, average age around 51. Postmenopause is a lower, steadier baseline where bone, muscle, heart and metabolic health move to the centre. Periods stopping before 45 need assessment; premature or early menopause is usually treated until the normal age.
  3. Recognizing perimenopause — three clusters. Menstrual: shorter cycles, longer gaps, heavier or lighter bleeding, spotting. Physical: hot flashes, night sweats, poor sleep, aches, palpitations, weight gain, vaginal dryness, painful sex, low libido, urinary symptoms. Cognitive and mood: anxiety, irritability or rage, brain fog, “I'm not myself.”
  4. You usually don't need a blood test. After 45, diagnosis is clinical. Hormones fluctuate wildly but inside the “normal” range, so a single lab can look fine while symptoms are real. Test when periods stop before 45, symptoms are atypical, or to rule out thyroid, anemia, pregnancy or medication effects.
  5. The treatment map is holistic. Lifestyle foundations regardless; then MHT, the most effective option for hot flashes and night sweats and protective for bone; non-hormonal medications; local vaginal therapies; targeted supports — CBT/CBT-I, mental health care, a bone plan.
  6. The WHI, reinterpreted. The 2002 alarm took outcomes from women mostly in their 60s and applied them to newly menopausal 50-year-olds; prescribing collapsed and women suffered untreated for years. Now: timing matters — under 60 or within 10 years of menopause the benefit–risk is favourable, while starting after 65 raises cardiovascular and dementia risk; transdermal estradiol and micronized progesterone carry lower clot and breast-cancer risk than the trial's formulations. “MHT is not for everyone — but it should not be dismissed for everyone either.”
  7. What MHT is and isn't. Estrogen, with progesterone if you have a uterus. Evidence-based for hot flashes and night sweats, sleep, genitourinary symptoms and bone. Not a weight-loss drug, not a guaranteed libido treatment, not contraception and not an anti-ageing protocol. Delivery: patch, gel or pill; progestogen by pill, combined patch or IUD; local vaginal cream, tablet or ring.
  8. Risks and who shouldn't. Blood clots, stroke, breast cancer, gallstones, endometrial cancer if estrogen is unopposed — risk varies with age, route, dose, duration and history. Cautions: past breast or endometrial cancer, unexplained bleeding, clotting disorder, severe liver disease, untreated hypertension. Reassess annually.
  9. Non-hormonal is not nothing. NK3-receptor antagonists (fezolinetant, elinzanetant); SSRIs/SNRIs; gabapentin or pregabalin third-line. CBT and CBT-I; lubricants and moisturizers locally.
  10. “Bioidentical” is a marketing word. Many regulated prescriptions are body-identical; the problem is pharmacy-compounded mixtures — no standardized dosing, quality control or trials, often expensive. She prescribes only regulated options.
  11. Symptom by symptom. Sleep and night sweats: triggers, cool room, CBT-I, treat the flashes. Mood and brain fog: treat sleep and flashes first; rule out depression, apnea, B12 or iron deficiency, thyroid, ADHD. Heavy or erratic periods: Mirena IUD first. Vaginal and urinary symptoms: local vaginal estrogen — low absorption, safe long-term, “not full-body hormones.” Low libido: fix pain first, then sleep, mood and medications; testosterone can be considered — avoid pellets and high-dose injections.
  12. Weight, muscle and GLP-1s. Central weight gain is real. Strength training two to three times a week is non-negotiable, protein 1–1.5 g/kg a day plus fibre. GLP-1s help some women but take muscle and bone unless paired with protein, resistance training, vitamin D and calcium.
  13. Bone and contraception. Bone loss accelerates in the first years after menopause: strength and balance training, vitamin D, calcium, DEXA when indicated. MHT is not contraception: continue birth control one year after the last period if over 50, two years if under 50.
  14. Health after menopause — a third of life. Prevent cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, central weight gain and type 2 diabetes. Checks: BP, cholesterol, A1c; breast screening yearly from 40, cervical every three years, bowel every two years from 50, bone density when indicated. Red flags: postmenopausal bleeding, a breast lump, blood in the stool, a change in bowel habit, unexplained weight loss, chest pain.

If symptoms are affecting your sleep, work, relationships or sex life, book the conversation — you don't need a blood test first.

Hormones and relationships

Dr. Darshan Shah moderating Drs. Kiran and Baldeep Bains · framed on stage as YPO-only and confidential; only the physicians' points appear here

“Perimenopause and the male decline usually land in the same house at the same time. Treat the couple, not the patient.”

  • 2 declines
    one household: her resilience, sleep and libido; his mood, body and empathy — at the same time
  • ~6 months
    when a man on TRT feels transformed — and, if his partner is untreated, when resentment starts
  • No stop at 60
    neither doctor takes patients off hormone therapy by age; monitoring decides
All eleven takeaways
  1. Two declines, one household. Falling progesterone and estrogen cut a woman's resilience, sleep and libido and can make her feel unrecognizable to herself; a man's slower testosterone decline brings irritability, body dissatisfaction, low libido and erectile trouble, and lowers his empathy just when it's needed. Dr. Shah's own admission: even as a physician, he wasn't as empathetic as he should have been when his wife hit perimenopause.
  2. Nobody recognizes themselves. Husbands ask Dr. Kiran to see wives who are “angry all the time”; the wives are just as confused. Women with low libido fear it will be read as disinterest or an affair. Men carry real shame around erectile dysfunction, and one failure becomes a performance-anxiety loop.
  3. What intimacy becomes. For many women in this stage, emotional security, patience and affection without expectation move to the front. Painful sex from vaginal dryness is treatable and is often the first thing to fix. Dr. Kiran now prefers the husband in the room for a low-libido consult.
  4. HRT is a couples therapy. A man on TRT typically feels transformed at about six months, and if his partner is untreated, mismatch and resentment follow. Treat both, together, and counsel the men to manage expectations. Both Bains said this was a learning point from their own practices.
  5. Testosterone is not symmetrical. It drives male libido decisively; in women it helps only partially. Dr. Shah's clinics have had success with the peptide kisspeptin in roughly half of women with low desire. Older men on TRT can drift into atrial fibrillation — dose reduction, not panic.
  6. Talk before the crisis. Couples rarely seek therapy until they're trying to save the marriage; Dr. Shah sends hormone patients to a couples therapist pre-emptively and rates the Gottman approach highly. Dr. Kiran's tools: a weekly sit-down, and letters for the things that are hard to say out loud.
  7. Compounded vs regulated, three views. Dr. Kiran prescribes only regulated products. Dr. Baldeep uses compounded testosterone creams because commercial strengths are too low. Dr. Shah's analogy: a supermarket Oreo is the same every time; a bakery Oreo depends on the baker — sometimes you need the bakery, but the doctor should have visited it first.
  8. Peptides are not hormones. Get estrogen, progesterone and testosterone right first; peptides are additive at best. Some are approved (GLP-1s, growth hormone); most are experimental, and physicians will not prescribe what Health Canada hasn't approved.
  9. There is no automatic stop at 60. Patients in their 70s and 80s stay on with monitoring as long as no new risk factors appear. Dosing is n-of-1: the relationship, subjective wellbeing and symptoms are all endpoints.
  10. Why your GP may not be up to speed. Menopause got a page in medical school and TRT got nothing. Arrive informed, and if your doctor isn't confident, ask for a referral. Both expect substantial data on estrogen, testosterone and Alzheimer's protection within five to seven years.
  11. One practical note for men on cream: apply to a low-hair area under the underwear line, not the genitals, to avoid transferring it to a partner.

Where the science is still open

Where the experts disagreed

Eight speakers, one weekend, and they did not all agree. That's the most useful part of the record — it shows where your own goals decide. Nobody said any of these to each other on stage; they emerge from reading the sessions side by side.

How much should you track?

One viewShah: collect everything — wearable, CGM, quarterly bloods, a chart in the closet. “You never know what patterns you'll find.”
The otherMorrison: a bad sleep score first thing validates a bad day. Kinley: check in with yourself, not your phone. Jones: if you feel good and your bloods are good, why chase numbers?
VerdictTrack to learn, then review on a schedule, not every morning. Jones' two-week review beats a daily readiness score.

How much should you test?

One viewShah: yearly calcium score (per the workbook), full-body MRI every 3–5 years, yearly Galleri, APOE and p-tau217 — find it at stage one.
The otherGillis: incidentalomas cause harm; ask whether the result would change treatment before you order it.
VerdictBoth would agree on one calcium score. Beyond that, decide with a physician who will act on the answer, not just report it.

Colon screening

One viewShah's workbook: colonoscopy every five years from 45.
The otherGillis and K. Bains: stool test every two years from 50; colonoscopy only with family history, blood, symptoms or genetic risk — it carries a perforation risk.
VerdictThe Canadian standard of care is the stool test. Ask for a scope if you have a reason; don't schedule one on a calendar.

Sleep: hours or timing?

One viewMorrison: seven hours of actual sleep shows the best longevity outcomes.
The otherJones: consistency of bed and wake time is trumping total hours in the newest research.
VerdictNot really in conflict — Morrison's CBT-I also anchors on a fixed wake time. Fix the timing first; the hours follow.

Alcohol

One viewShah: the liver handles one drink per two hours; cap at 1–2 drinks, 2–3 days a week.
The otherJones: no safe level exists, but relationship depth is the strongest longevity predictor and rigid abstinence can cost you that. Gillis: “drink less.”
VerdictShah gives the ceiling; Jones gives the reason not to make it a rule you resent. Tie it to your goals and your bloodwork.

Compounded hormones

One viewKiran Bains: never — no standardized dose, no safety data, often expensive.
The otherBaldeep Bains: commercial testosterone gels are too weak, so he compounds and manages by feedback. Shah: sometimes you need the bakery, but vet it.
VerdictA married couple disagreeing on stage is the honest state of the field. Regulated first; compounded only from a pharmacy your doctor has actually visited.

Pellets

One viewShah's workbook lists BHRT as “topical, injectable, or pellet therapy.”
The otherBaldeep Bains: no — too variable, can't be adjusted, infection risk. Kiran Bains: avoid pellets and high-dose injections.
VerdictThe two physicians who would actually treat you here both say no.

Hormone targets

One viewShah: optimize men toward ~900 ng/dL; most women he knows are on E+P.
The otherKiran Bains: after 45 you usually don't need a blood test at all — treat symptoms, not a number. Baldeep Bains: “numbers are a guide, not a verdict.”
VerdictThe Halifax doctors are more conservative than the keynote. Symptoms plus a physician who monitors beat a target number.

Supplements

One viewShah: a core stack of four or five, and creatine for everyone.
The otherJones: supplements are the last item on the 80/20 list. “Creatine is not going to change your life.”
VerdictSequence, not contradiction. Basics first; Shah's stack once the basics are in place.

Zone 2 cardio

One viewShah on stage: skips it entirely for lack of time; sprint intervals only.
The otherShah's own workbook: “Train zone 2 ~30 minutes per day. Build the aerobic base.” Jones: a 5–10 minute slow jog carried ~30% lower all-cause mortality in the trial he cited.
VerdictEven the keynote disagrees with himself here, which tells you it's a time-budget question, not a science one. If you have the time, easy cardio counts; if you don't, intervals.
